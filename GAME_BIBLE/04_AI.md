# 04 — AI

## Approach

**Waypoint following on authored racing lines.** No pathfinding, no navmesh, no
steering-behaviour stack. This is a deliberate scope decision — AI navigation is a
listed project risk, and waypoint AI on a bounded track is a solved problem.

AI drivers use the **same vehicle physics as the player**. They do not cheat with
different acceleration curves or ignore surfaces. Difficulty comes from decision
quality and input precision, not from separate rules.

This matters: if an AI car can do something the player's car cannot, players notice,
and the racing stops feeling fair.

## Racing lines

Each track defines one or more racing lines as ordered waypoint lists.

```js
{
  id: "main",
  waypoints: [
    { x: 420, y: 180, targetSpeed: 1.0, width: 46 },
    { x: 610, y: 210, targetSpeed: 0.7, width: 38 },
    // ...
  ]
}
```

- `targetSpeed` — fraction of the vehicle's max speed. Authored per corner.
- `width` — lateral tolerance. Wide sections let AI spread out and race each other;
  narrow sections force single file.

Alternate lines (shortcuts, high-risk routes) are separate lists with entry and exit
waypoints that rejoin the main line. See `05_Tracks.md`.

## Driver loop

Each frame, an AI driver:

1. Finds its target waypoint (current + lookahead scaled by speed).
2. Steers toward it, with lateral offset for personality and traffic avoidance.
3. Brakes if approaching a waypoint whose `targetSpeed` is below current speed.
4. Drifts if the required turn rate exceeds what grip allows.
5. Decides on boost (see below).
6. Checks for obstacles ahead; nudges the lateral offset to avoid.
7. Detects being stuck; triggers recovery.

### Step 6 was specified for six phases before it existed

It went unbuilt and unnoticed because every bedroom track keeps its props at the
edges, where a driver on the line never meets one. Putting buckets in the road on
Bucket Brigade found it in a single race: the field drove into them lap after lap.

| | before | after | baseline (rug) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bucket Brigade | 124.5s, 27 impacts, 2029 stuck ticks | 91.6s, 8.7, 206 | 92.5s, 6, 105 |
| The Big Dig | 92.2s, 14.3, 601 | 80.4s, 5.3, 185 | — |

`avoidProps()` shifts the desired lateral offset until it misses everything ahead,
picking whichever side needs less movement so a driver already going one way
commits rather than dithering across the middle, and clamping to the road so it
never dodges into a wall. Props being jumped — `v.z > p.clearAt` — are ignored,
which is what lets a driver launched off a ramp sail over a bucket instead of
swerving round one it was about to clear.

**The Big Dig also exposed a bug in `sandReading`.** The scan asked for a full sand
cell either side regardless of the road; on a 230-wide trench with unjumpable
walls that had the driver aiming 70 units off the line with 115 to play with, and
grinding along the wall for a 63-second lap. `SAND_CLEARANCE` now keeps the scan
off the kerb, and a road too narrow for a full-cell scan returns no opinion at all
rather than a confident wrong one. Wide tracks had hidden it completely.

## Personalities

Personalities are **parameter sets**, not separate code paths. Each is a modifier
bundle over the base driver.

| Personality | Behaviour | Key parameters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Speedster** | Very fast on straights, frequently crashes | High `targetSpeedMul`, late braking, low `cornerCaution` |
| **The Technician** | Clean lines, efficient drifts | High `lineAccuracy`, optimal drift timing, high `boostEfficiency` |
| **The Bully** | Heavy vehicle, pushes others aside | High `aggression`, seeks contact, ignores lateral avoidance |
| **The Explorer** | Frequently takes alternate routes | High `shortcutBias`, accepts higher risk |
| **The Rookie** | Slow and forgiving, for beginner events | Low `targetSpeedMul`, early braking, rare boost use |

```js
const PERSONALITY = {
  targetSpeedMul:  1.0,   // scales authored corner speeds
  lineAccuracy:    0.8,   // 0..1, how tightly the line is held
  cornerCaution:   1.0,   // braking distance multiplier
  aggression:      0.3,   // willingness to make contact
  shortcutBias:    0.3,   // probability of taking an alternate route
  boostEfficiency: 0.7,   // how well boost is timed
  mistakeChance:   0.05,  // per-corner chance of a deliberate error
};
```

**Deliberate mistakes are a feature.** An AI that never errs is unbeatable-feeling
even when slow. `mistakeChance` produces overshoots and missed apexes that let
players pass and feel good about it.

### Reading the surface — `sandReading`

A ninth parameter, added with Sandbox Speedway. It is the only trait that reads
the **world** rather than the authored line: on sand, the driver scans across the
road and steers toward ground that has already been packed down (`SandGrid.js`).
Inert everywhere else, because the grid is inactive on non-sandy tracks.

| Personality | `sandReading` | reading it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technician | 0.95 | reading the surface *is* the personality |
| Bully | 0.55 | will happily take the line you just made |
| Speedster | 0.45 | too busy going fast to look down |
| Rookie | 0.15 | hasn't noticed the ground is different |

**This does not break the no-cheating rule.** Compaction is drawn on screen, so
the AI is looking at the ground exactly as a player does. It gets no lap times,
no opponent state, and no lookahead the player lacks.

Measured on Dune Dash, comparing laps 2-3 against the same race with the trait
zeroed: Technician −1.0s, Bully −0.5s, Speedster −0.6s, Rookie −0.2s. The gain
tracks the parameter, which is what tells you the trait is doing the work and
not something else.

A side effect worth keeping: it **breaks up single file**. The Technician's
lateral spread went from 1.3 to 23.1 units, because it now picks a line instead
of tracing one. The wander in step 2 of the driver loop exists to stop the field
running nose-to-tail; on sand this does the same job for a reason.

### The player started on pole (Phase 9)

"It still feels too easy" was raised twice, which makes it a bug rather than a
preference. The first report was specific: *"once you pass the opponents, it's
not hard to end up even lapping some of them."* That is not a claim about pace.
It is a claim about the race being over at the first pass.

It was. **Humans took `grid[0]` upward and opponents filled in behind, so the
player started every single race in the lead.** There was nothing to overtake;
the field simply fell away. Measured across nine skill and difficulty
combinations on a six-opponent race, the player was in front within **6-8
seconds** and spent **0-1 seconds** of a ninety-second race behind anybody.

Two earlier attempts at this had tuned the field's pace, which could never have
worked: the problem was never how fast the opponents were.

**The field's calibration cannot be moved to fix it.** `normal` difficulty paired
with the Technician is the reference every target time in the game is measured
against, so retuning it would silently invalidate eighteen events. Grid position
is the lever that leaves that alone — and it was not a tunable at all until now.

`PLAYER_START_BEHIND` is a fraction of the field: 0 is pole, 1 is dead last.
Swept against the same nine combinations:

| | Easy | Normal | Hard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| pole | wins, leads by 22-42s | wins, leads by 7s | wins, leads by 7s |
| **mid-grid** | **wins, leads by 13-43s** | **only the strongest wins, from 38s** | **3rd, 3rd, 2nd** |
| dead last | 1.00 loses | nobody wins | 4th-5th |

Mid-grid is the only setting where the ladder reads as a ladder. Dead last is the
arcade convention, but the field's pace was calibrated when the requirement was
"must not be caught", and from the back even a strong player cannot win on
Normal.

The player stand-in is an AI with a raised top speed, which **understates** a
real player: it takes no better line, finds no shortcuts, and spends boost no
more cleverly. Erring on the hard side of that is deliberate given the
complaint.

Everything downstream of the start had to follow: the camera snapped to
`grid[0]`, which is now somebody else's car, and `reset()` put `vehicles[i]`
back on `grid[i]`, which was only ever right while humans started at the front —
a retry would have quietly restored the old layout. Each car now records the
slot it actually started from.

**All twelve standard-race platinum times were re-measured**, because a race now
includes time spent in traffic. Time trials are untouched: they are solo, so the
player is still alone at the front.

#### It left two cars in the same box (Phase 10)

Moving humans off pole broke the loop that seats the opponents, which still
counted from the front with `grid[i]`. While the player really was behind
everybody those ranges could not overlap; at mid-grid they do. Measured on Rug
Route, the player and the Blue Buggy spawned at **identical coordinates, 0.0
units apart**, with five of the eight slots empty. Opponents now fill the slots
the humans are not in.

The cost was not only cosmetic. A race that opens by shoving two interpenetrating
cars apart resolves differently depending on which way they happen to squirt, and
that showed up as variance: across five seeds the first Ice Rink race spread
**6.52 seconds**, against under 2.3 everywhere else on the board. With the slots
unique it spreads **1.28**, and the mean spread across all eighteen events falls
from 1.09 to 0.96.

That is the argument for watching the spread column and not just the median. The
median never looked wrong — 92.75 before, 92.90 after — while the event underneath
it was quietly the least reproducible thing in the game.

### Betting on a fork — `gateNerve`

A tenth parameter, added with switching gates (`07_World_Stunt_Track.md`). A
gate offers two rides from one mouth and the branch you get is decided by the
lane you are in, so an AI expresses the choice through the lateral offset it
already steers to. Inert on tracks without a gate.

The bet is on **arrival** speed, not current speed: a car approaching a mouth is
usually still accelerating, and judging the expensive branch on what it is doing
right now makes every driver too timid. `gateNerve` is how optimistic that bet
is.

| Personality | `gateNerve` | arrives at | takes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speedster | 1.35 | 391 | the high road, and would try it slower |
| Technician | 1.00 | 345 | the high road, knowing it will make it |
| Bully | 0.90 | 335 | the high road |
| Rookie | 0.55 | 316 | the low road |

Measured on Shelf Run, whose high branch demands 320 at the mouth.

**The first version had the whole field taking the same branch**, which makes a
fork a corner with extra steps. The decision was being made 620 units out, where
every car is still climbing towards its terminal speed, so the estimate needed a
fudge large enough to clear the entry fee for anybody — including the Rookie, at
which point the trait does nothing. A driver needs about 180 units to ease 70
across the road, so deciding at **340** leaves room to move while judging a
speed close to the one it will actually turn up with.

The decision is **committed** for as long as the gate is ahead. Re-evaluating it
every tick makes a driver straddle the divider at the moment its speed crosses
the threshold, and arrive in neither lane.

Overriding rather than blending is deliberate: wander and `sandReading` are
preferences about *where on the road* to be, and a gate is a decision about
*which road*. Splitting the difference between two branches takes neither.

**Sizing the scan is not free.** The first implementation sampled a fraction of
the racing line's usable width — 134 units on Dune Dash — which put the entire
five-point scan inside a single 70-unit sand cell. Every sample returned the same
number, half the reads came back "no opinion", and the AI drove straight past
packed sand it was alongside. **A scan narrower than one grid cell cannot resolve
anything by construction.** The radius is now taken from `SandGrid.CELL`, not
from the road.

## Difficulty

Difficulty scales the personality bundle, it does not replace it. A Speedster on
Easy is still recognisably a Speedster.

| | Easy | Normal | Hard |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Opponent speed | Reduced | Balanced | Faster lines |
| Shortcut use | Rare | Moderate | Aggressive |
| Boost timing | Poor | Decent | Effective |
| Player recovery | Stronger | Standard | Smaller advantage |
| Collision handling | More forgiving | Standard | Standard |
| `mistakeChance` | Elevated | Baseline | Low |

**Player-selectable from Phase 5**, and the player's choice overrides whatever an
event was authored with. Pacing across the roster is carried by lap count and
grid size, which a difficulty setting does not touch, so nothing is lost by
letting the setting win.

"More forgiving collision handling" and "stronger player recovery" are one
mechanism: a per-vehicle `forgiveness` multiplier, applied to the **player only**.
Opponents keep normal physics on every setting, so Easy reads as the player being
tougher rather than as rivals being limp.

**Platinum is not awarded on Easy.** Gold and below are unaffected — the point of
an easier setting is to be able to finish and progress — but the mastery tier has
to mean the same thing for everyone.

## Dynamic assistance

> **Built in Phase 5.** Trailing AI get up to +6% target speed, scaled by how far
> back they are. A leading car is **never** slowed — the multiplier is clamped at
> or above 1.0, verified. A trailing player earns boost slightly faster. The
> whole system is disabled in Time Trial.

Subtle rubber-banding for players who fall far behind. Must never be detectable.

Rules:

- **Only applies when the player is behind**, never to hold a leading player back.
  Slowing a winning player is the single most resented mechanic in arcade racing.
- Trailing AI receive a small speed bonus so the pack does not string out
  indefinitely; leading AI receive **no** penalty.
- The player's own boost regeneration may increase slightly when far behind.
- Maximum effect should be small enough that a skilled player still wins comfortably
  and a struggling player still loses — it compresses the field, it does not decide
  the race.
- **Disabled entirely in Time Trial** and any mode with leaderboards.

Suggested cap: ±6% effective speed. Tune with real players; err low.

## Stuck detection and recovery

An AI is stuck if it has not advanced a waypoint for ~2 seconds while grounded.

Recovery: reverse briefly, re-aim at the current waypoint, resume. If still stuck
after a second attempt, respawn at the last checkpoint exactly as the player would.
Never let an AI grind against a wall for a whole lap — it looks broken and it is.

## Overtaking

Lightweight. When an AI is within about two car lengths of a slower vehicle ahead:

1. Pick a side with more track width available.
2. Apply a lateral offset to the racing line.
3. Hold the offset until clear, then rejoin.
4. If blocked and `aggression` is high, make contact instead.

No cooperative traffic model, no lane reservation. Overtaking that occasionally
fails is more entertaining than overtaking that always works.

## Pace, rebalanced (Phase 8) — the field was lappable

Play feedback: *"feels a bit too easy, and once you pass the opponents it's not
hard to end up lapping some of them."* Measured, and true.

**Finish times cannot be used to judge this.** A race ends once the humans are
home, and everyone still out is stamped with a shared time — three AI reporting
an identical 93.7s looks like a photo finish and means nothing. The honest
measure is **solo pace**: one car, no traffic, best lap.

That exposed one car dragging the floor down:

| solo best lap | before | after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rookie (Yellow Rocket) | **36.1s** | 32.1s |
| Bully | 30.8s | 30.1s |
| Technician | 30.1s | **30.1s — unchanged** |
| Speedster | 29.1s | 27.8s |
| field spread | 24.1% | 15.5% |

A 7-second-per-lap deficit is ~21s over three laps: most of a lap on a 90-second
race, which is exactly the lapping being reported. Being the slowest in the field
is the Rookie's job; being seven seconds a lap slower is not a difficulty setting
but the absence of one. Lap risk is now **0.40 of a lap over three** — passable,
not lappable.

**The Technician on normal did not move, deliberately.** Every target time in
`events.js` is calibrated against that exact pairing, so shifting it would
silently invalidate eighteen events' worth of medals. Difficulty was adjusted at
the ends instead: easy 0.86 → 0.90, hard 1.06 → 1.15 with fewer mistakes and
more boost. Verified after the change: rug-route-01 reference 91.7s against the
92.3s it was calibrated on — inside noise.

### A difficulty setting must never make a car slower

Hard at 1.15 made the **Speedster slower**: 29.5s against 27.8s on normal.
Personality, difficulty and catch-up all multiply the corner target, and
unbounded they ask a driver to carry more speed than the authored racing line
can hold — so it overshoots, scrubs, and loses more than the setting gave it.

`AGGRO_CAP` (1.18) clamps the product. It sits just above a Technician on hard
(0.99 × 1.15 = 1.139), so cautious drivers get the full benefit and only the
already-overdriving ones are held back. Hard now runs a 5.3% spread with its
fastest at 28.1s, against normal's 27.8s and a 15.5% spread — a tight quick
field rather than a slow one with a 6% lift.

## The item personalities were backwards (Phase 10)

Open question 2 asked whether the AI should use items once items existed. It
already did — `AIDriver` had an `itemSkill` trait per personality and a block
that sets `input.item`. Nobody had ever counted whether it fired.

**It fires.** Measured over a full item race, all eight opponents picked items
up and all eight used them, 25 stuns landed. So far so good.

**But the personalities did the opposite of what they say.** The fallback was
`itemHold > 6 * itemSkill`, which fires SOONEST for the LOWEST skill — against
the Rookie's own comment, *"sits on things and fires them late"*. Measured:
low-skill drivers fired 11.5 items on average, high-skill 9.7.

### One trait could not describe them

The four documented behaviours are **not monotonic in skill**:

| | says | so it needs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technician 0.95 | "waits for a target, then uses it" | good eyes, real patience |
| Bully 0.80 | "saves the offensive ones for company" | good eyes, real patience |
| Speedster 0.60 | "empties the slot the moment it fills" | **middling eyes, no patience** |
| Rookie 0.25 | "sits on things and fires them late" | **poor eyes, huge patience** |

The Speedster is mid-skill and impatient; the Rookie is low-skill and endlessly
patient. No single number produces both. So `itemSkill` now means only **how far
off a rival registers as a target**, and a separate `itemPatience` is how long a
driver holds before firing blind.

### A target is someone in FRONT

The first attempt still failed, and the failure was informative. Targeting used
`nearestRival`, which is omnidirectional — and in an eight-car pack somebody is
nearly always within a few hundred units in *some* direction. The Technician
therefore never had to wait and fired the instant it was handed anything: a
**0.02s** median hold.

Two of the three offensive items travel forward (the aeroplane flies along the
car's heading, the hammer reaches 210), so a rival behind is not a target at
all. `rivalAhead` requires the rival to be in front and roughly in the cone the
item would fly through. That is what makes waiting mean something.

### Measured, median hold on an offensive item

| | skill | patience | before | after |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speedster | 0.60 | 0.4 | 0.32s | **0.42s** |
| Bully | 0.80 | 4.5 | 4.52s | **2.30s** |
| Technician | 0.95 | 4.0 | 0.02s | **4.02s** |
| Rookie | 0.25 | 7.0 | 7.02s | **7.02s** |

The Bully firing sooner than the Technician is emergent rather than designed:
it spends more of the race in traffic, so its targets actually appear, while the
Technician more often leads and times out at its patience instead.

**No new information is read.** `rivalAhead` uses positions already on screen,
so the AI still does nothing a player could not.
